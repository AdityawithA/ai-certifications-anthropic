## What the Course Is About

This course isn't about *how* to use an AI tool — it's about building an accurate mental model of what AI systems can and can't actually do. The core idea: most people's intuitions about AI are miscalibrated in one of two directions — either wildly overconfident ("it's basically a person, it knows everything") or dismissively skeptical ("it's just autocomplete, it's useless for real work"). Neither is accurate, and both lead to bad decisions. The course exists to recalibrate that intuition so you can decide, task by task, when to trust AI output and when to double-check it yourself.

## Key Concepts

### 1. Where AI Genuinely Excels
- Pattern recognition across large amounts of text/data
- Drafting, summarizing, and rephrasing at speed
- Working across many domains at once (breadth over deep specialization)
- Consistency on repetitive, well-defined tasks
- Brainstorming and generating options quickly

### 2. Where AI Falls Short
- **Hallucination** — generating plausible-sounding but false information, especially for obscure facts, citations, or numbers
- **No real-time awareness** — knowledge is frozen at a training cutoff unless given external tools/search
- **Shallow reasoning under pressure** — can miss logical inconsistencies, especially in long or multi-step problems
- **No genuine understanding of consequences** — it doesn't "know" what's actually true or false, it predicts likely text
- **Sensitivity to how a question is framed** — different phrasing of the same question can produce different answers

### 3. Calibrating Trust
A central theme: trust should scale with **verifiability and stakes**, not with how confident the AI sounds. High-stakes or hard-to-verify claims (legal, medical, financial, factual citations) need human review. Low-stakes, easily-checked, or purely generative tasks (first drafts, brainstorming, formatting) are safer to hand off fully.

### 4. Working *With* Limitations, Not Around Them
Instead of trying to "fix" AI's limitations, the course frames the skill as designing your workflow around them:
- Ask for sources and verify them independently
- Break big tasks into smaller, checkable steps
- Use AI for the first draft, not the final word
- Treat AI output as a *starting point* for judgment, not a replacement for it

## What I Learned

- AI capability isn't uniform — it's *task-dependent*, so "is AI good?" is the wrong question. The right question is "is AI good *at this specific task*?"
- Confidence in an AI's tone is not a reliable signal of accuracy — fluent, well-structured answers can still be wrong.
- The biggest practical risk isn't AI being wrong — it's *not noticing* when it's wrong because the output looks polished.
- Good AI use is less about prompting tricks and more about knowing which tasks to delegate, which to double-check, and which to keep entirely human.

## One-Line Takeaway
> AI is a powerful tool for tasks with checkable outputs and low stakes for error — the skill isn't using AI well, it's knowing where its limits are before you rely on it.
